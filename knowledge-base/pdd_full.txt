Appendix 7: BCU Calculation Methodology

As a relatively small, distributed operation, Charm relies on multiple operating activities which require the use of diesel fuel:
Transportation via road and rail
Other rolling stock such as telehandlers and front-end loaders
Onsite generators where grid power is not available

Our options for addressing the emissions impact of this fuel usage are limited. For transportation, our scale requires us to rely on third-party transportation providers who use fossil diesel. Our ability to use renewable diesel (RD) for other applications is also limited by availability as none of our operations occur within an LCFS jurisdiction. As a result, supply of renewable diesel is not practically accessible for our on-site applications.

The most expedient means of addressing the impact of diesel required by our operations, then, is the procurement of renewable diesel emissions attributes via Book and Claim Units (BCUs). By purchasing credibly additional BCUs, we finance the production of RD that would not be economically feasible to produce absent the BCU purchase. This is delivered outside of an LCFS jurisdiction, displacing volumes of fossil diesel that would have been used in a counterfactual scenario.

The BCUs generated by the production and delivery of the fuel are associated with a specific quantity of avoided emissions estimated based on the volume of fuel delivered. This is documented by a Proof of Sustainability (PoS) certificate provided for the volumes delivered. The PoS certificate accounts for the Well-to-Tank (WTT) emissions impact of the fuel production from feedstock through production and ultimately delivery. This assessment is conducted in accordance with and certified by a reputable existing framework (RSB Global). The Tank-to-Wheel (TTW) emissions of the fuel usage are quantified using the GLEC emission factor for the appropriate fuel associated with the BCUs. This allows us to account for the full Well-to-Wheel (WTW) emissions for the production, distribution, and use of the RD whose attributes are purchased as BCUs.

As already noted in the PDD, our fossil diesel emissions are also calculated using WTW emission factors. Transport emissions are calculated using the Distance Based Method outlined in the existing Transportation Emissions Accounting Module, while onsite usage is calculated based on the total quantity of fuel delivered to the site as reflected by invoices. These values are scaled by the applicable WTW emission factor from GLEC in order to quantify the total emissions associated with production, distribution, and use of the fuel.

In each case, the use of WTW emission factors allows for a straightforward comparison between total emissions generated as a consequence of fossil diesel usage and those generated by renewable diesel. The renewable diesel displaces the use of fossil diesel, and the demonstration of additionality required by the Transportation Emissions Accounting Module for BCUs establishes that displacement would not have occurred in a counterfactual scenario where the BCU was not purchased by the project. Therefore, the difference between the WTW emissions of a given quantity of fossil diesel and renewable diesel constitute the quantity of emissions avoided as a result of the purchase of BCUs associated with the renewable diesel.

With this as the background, Charm is using a calculation method that is different from the equations in 4.2.1 of the Transportation Emissions Accounting Module. The Transportation Emissions Accounting Module requires LCA calculations to be done in a manner that simulates the direct replacement of FD by RD by volume for operations, which means the LCA acknowledges the emissions associated with the RD value chain. The alternate calculation used by Charm results in BCUs being retired to address emissions from the RD volume used, and the associated value chain emissions, in the LCA calculation. We can document that our BCU purchases are additional and can account for the WTW emissions of both fossil and renewable diesel using emission factors from reputable sources. Therefore, we have a reliable quantity of avoided emissions associated with our BCU purchases that will allow us to transparently quantify the impact of avoided emissions from the RD value chain.

Our methodology is to match the emissions impact in terms of kg CO2e emitted with the avoided emissions of BCUs purchased and retired. We will purchase and retire a quantity of BCUs whose avoided emissions will be less than or equal to the emissions generated as a result of fossil diesel usage. This enables a reduction of diesel emissions that are netted against removals in a GHG statement by the quantity of avoided emissions attributable to the RD associated with purchased and retired BCUs.

This is accounted for using the following set of equations:

Equation for Well-to-Tank Emissions for Renewable Diesel
EPRD, WTT = CIRD, WTT * LHVRD, WTT
Where:
EPRD,WTT is the WTT emission profile for each kilogram of a given batch or delivery of RD
CIRD, WTT is the WTT carbon intensity given in the PoS denominated in grams of CO2e per MJ of fuel
LHVRD, WTT is the lower heating value given in the PoS denominated in MJ per kg of fuel

Equation for Well-to-Wheel Emissions for Renewable Diesel
EPRD, WTW = (EPRD, WTT + EFRD, TTW)
Where:
EPRD, WTW is the WTW emission profile for each kilogram of a given batch or delivery of RD
EPRD,WTT is the WTT emission profile for each kilogram of a given batch or delivery of RD
EFRD, TTW is the TTW emission factor for the applicable type of fuel (currently sourced from GLEC)

Equation for Renewable Diesel WTW Avoided Emissions
AERD = EFFD, WTW  - EPRD, WTW
Where:
AERD is the avoided emissions from the use of a given quantity of RD
EFFD, WTW is the WTW emission factor quantifying the emission impact in CO2e for each kilogram of fossil diesel (currently sourced from GLEC)
EPRD, WTW is the WTW emission profile quantifying the emission impact in CO2e for each kilogram of renewable diesel associated with a given batch or delivery

Equation for Netting Fossil Diesel Emissions against Avoided Emissions from Renewable Diesel BCUs
Net CO2eFD = (CO2eTransport + CO2eOn-Site) - (AERD * MassRD)
Where:
Net CO2eFD is the net CO2e attributable to the use of fossil diesel which will be deducted from the gross sequestration in a given GHG Statement
CO2eTransport is the total CO2e attributable to transport activities, typically calculated using the Distance-Based Method provided in the Transportation Emissions Accounting module
CO2eOn-Site is the total CO2e attributable to on-site (non-transport) activities, typically calculated by applying a WTW emission factor to the quantity of fuel delivered to the site for project activities
AERD is the avoided emissions from the use of a given quantity of RD
MassRD is the mass of RD whose attributes have been procured and are being retired by the project in order to offset FD emissions in a given GHG Statement
Note: Net CO2eFD may only be a positive value or zero. It may never be a negative value.



Appendix 8 – Basco 6 EW Bio-oil Sequestration Site Permanence Monitoring and Permit Compliance Plan
Supporting data here

The Basco 6 EW is a former saltwater disposal well located in Evangeline Parish, Louisiana. It was drilled in 2011, and while active, injected nearly 20,000 bbls of produced water before transitioning to orphan well status in 2016. Charm gained permission to test the well’s integrity in February 2024, and finding the well in suitable condition, requested liability from the State of Louisiana to repurpose the well for permanent sequestration of bio-oil.

The data acquisition and monitoring plan framed below highlights how Charm will fulfill Isometric’s Bio-oil Storage in Permeable Reservoirs (“Protocol”) module with a single injection asset (“Well”) in addition to those anticipated from a Class V UIC permit issued by the Louisiana Department of Energy and Natural Resources (“LDENR”).

The following deviations from the Protocol are required on account of the project design; see discussion below for alternative methods, where applicable:

While the Protocol accommodates the presence of a monitoring well, the Basco 6 EW project consists only of a single Well for injection. As a result, formation fluid analytes (i.e., pH, density, anions/cations, etc.) will not be available after bio-oil injection operations commence, as doing so would jeopardize previously sequestered bio-oil. Bio-oil density will be referenced relative to that of the in-situ formation fluid at baseline conditions. In addition, formation fluid will not be available for corrosion monitoring purposes. See Section 3.1.1 and Section 3.2.
Bio-oil analytes at each injection batch will not include isotopes or geochemical characterization (“fingerprint”) of bio-oil compounds unless mandated by LDENR, as referenced in Section 3.1.1. This data is both time and cost prohibitive to conduct routinely, and does not inform project permanence.
Corrosion monitoring as described in Section 3.1.2 will be continuously monitored by tubing and annulus pressure, with visual inspection performed on tubing and/or packer only if and when the tubing and/or packer is run out of the hole for maintenance. Pulling the equipment from the well for the purpose of visually monitoring this equipment every 6 months unnecessarily jeopardizes the well’s completion and the equipment’s condition. It is also time and cost prohibitive, requiring LDENR approval before the work is scheduled with an on site MIPT to be witnessed by LDENR staff upon completion of the work. 
Charm continues to explore and mature material compatibility testing with a wide range of steel alloys and bio-oils to inform longevity of J-55 and other well-specific materials.
Direct measurement of bio-oil migration as described in Section 3.1.3 is not possible without use of a monitoring well(s). Plume development will be inferred from the Warner-Lehr model, but is not expected to be required by LDENR. Vertical migration will be monitored annually via radioactive tracer survey.
Groundwater monitoring as described in Section 3.1.3 is not anticipated to be required by LDENR.
Continuous inline gas detectors equipped with sensors for GHG and VOCs specified in the Protocol (Section 3.1.3) are prohibitively expensive for incorporating into Charm’s project. The unlikely occurrence of gas to surface will be monitored as described below:
Continuous annulus pressure monitoring will detect any subsurface tubing or casing leakage. In the event of an unexplained annulus pressure reading, gas detection sensors and/or cameras will be used to determine the presence of any gas leakage and corrective action applied where root cause analysis justifies.
Every six months, a wellhead gas measurement will be conducted and compared with baseline readings as described in the Protocol.
Annual mechanical integrity testing will confirm that the well construction materials are sound as described in the Protocol.
A seismic monitoring program as described in Section 3.1.3 is not expected to be required by LDENR.
Charm’s understanding of bio-oil compatibility has been enhanced by external research which identified calcium as a common reservoir mineral most sensitive to bio-oil; work is underway to extend this research to the Lower Miocene formation sampled from the Basco 6 EW well. In addition, we are leveraging this learning in our candidate selection process as a primary means of mitigating, if not eliminating, bio-oil reactivity. Monitoring for potential reactivity will be inferred from continuously recorded surface pressure and injectivity trends. Periodic falloff tests will also inform reservoir quality changes resulting from either aging (e.g., increased “skin effect”) or reactivity (decreasing “skin effect”). Even in the event of stimulation-scale dissolution within the wellbore, a wellbore maintained to regulatory standards will not generate leakage.

Appendix 9: Basco 6 Bio-Oil Sequestration Site Well Closure and Remediation Plan
Location: Basco 6 EW, Evangeline Parish, Louisiana
Preliminary Work and Notifications
Prior to initiating the closure of the injection well, the operator shall notify the Louisiana Department of Natural Resources (LDNR) Office of Conservation by submitting a completed Form UIC-23. This notification must be provided at least ten days in advance of the intended start date of plugging and abandonment (P&A) operations. As part of the preparation, a mechanical integrity test (MIT) will be conducted to evaluate the condition of the well and confirm the integrity of the casing and tubing. If issues are detected, they will be documented and incorporated into the final plugging design.
A review of all available well records will be undertaken to inform the P&A approach. This review will include analysis of drilling and completion reports, cement bond logs (CBL), casing and tubing schematics, and any historical workover documentation. These data will guide decisions about plug placement and the required cement volumes to ensure a secure and permanent abandonment.
After the well has reached the end of injection, but prior to commencing P&A operations, the well will be shut-in and monitored to ensure bio-oil is stable. Once stability is verified, the well will be cleared for preparation to start plugging operations.
Wellbore Preparation
The plugging operations will begin with the removal of any downhole equipment, including injection tubing and the packer, to ensure clear access to the wellbore. Once these components are extracted, the well will be circulated with clean fluid to displace residual injection fluids and suspended solids from the wellbore. Wireline logs will be run in accordance with the UIC permit, including corrosion inspection and final vertical saturation. A caliper log will also be run to assess the internal diameter and condition of the casing to verify it can support planned plug placements without risk of washout or collapse.
Plugging Operations
The plugging plan for this well includes the strategic placement of cement plugs throughout the wellbore to isolate any perforated zones, protect underground sources of drinking water (USDW), and secure the surface. At the base of the well, a cement plug will be set to cover the bottom perforation or the base of the injection zone, with a typical plug length of at least 50 feet. A second plug will be placed across the casing shoe or the base of the USDW, extending for approximately 100 feet to ensure zonal isolation.
A surface plug will then be placed across the casing shoe, generally from 50 feet below to 50 feet above the shoe, for a total length of 100 feet. Finally, a shallow surface seal plug will be installed within the top 25 feet of the well to provide a protective barrier near the ground surface. All cement used will conform to API Class A specifications and may be modified with additives such as retarders or accelerators to accommodate specific well conditions, such as temperature or pressure.
Casing and Surface Equipment
Following the installation of cement plugs, the production casing will be cut approximately five feet below ground level using mechanical cutting tools. All surface equipment, including the wellhead and associated valves, will be removed to eliminate any above-ground hazards. A steel plate will be welded over the exposed casing stub to serve as a permanent barrier. In cases where venting is necessary, a vented cap may be installed instead. The site will then be secured, and appropriate signage will be installed to mark the well location for future reference.
Site Remediation
Upon completion of plugging activities, the well pad and surrounding site will be reclaimed. Grading operations will restore the terrain to its original contours or to a condition compatible with surrounding land use. All debris and materials from the P&A operation will be removed from the location. To encourage environmental recovery, the site will be reseeded with an approved native vegetation mix.
If required by site history or proximity to sensitive receptors, soil and groundwater samples may be collected to test for residual hydrocarbons, chlorides, or other contaminants associated with historical injection activities. These samples will be analyzed and results submitted to the relevant regulatory agencies to confirm that environmental remediation standards have been met.
Final Reporting
Following the successful completion of well plugging and site remediation, a Plug and Abandonment Report will be submitted to the LDNR using Form UIC-24. This report will include details such as the depth and composition of each cement plug, the results of pressure testing, and any supporting logs or diagrams generated during the operation. The report will also include photographic documentation and post-operation site descriptions as necessary. The operator will request that the well status be formally updated to "P&A" in the SONRIS database maintained by the LDNR.


Appendix 10: Basco 6 Supplementary Subsurface Characterization

Basco 6 EW

Confining layer(s) characterization
The perforated Miocene sands are underlain and overlain by thick marine mudstones and intraformational shales that provide low-transmissivity barriers. Log character (GR/spectral GR and density-neutron) indicates laterally continuous shale packages bracketing each sand lobe; these, together with multiple intra-sand baffles, hydraulically isolate the injection interval from the lowest USDW (~1,170’ MD). No free gas saturation is indicated on cased-hole logs, and baseline gases are trace-level only, consistent with aqueous solution gas rather than mobile gas.
Faults and transmissivity
There is no mapped faulting at the well or immediate lease based on available well control; however, seismic control was not licensed. Regionally, Louisiana’s onshore growth-fault trends are best developed toward the coast (e.g., Baton Rouge–Denham Springs–Scotlandville system) and are associated with Miocene–Pliocene strata at ~1,200–2,000 ft in the Baton Rouge aquifer system; inland “central Louisiana” occurrences such as at the Basco 6 EW location are fewer and smaller in throw than coastal growth faults [3, 4]. In the absence of site seismic, we assume no transmissive fault intersecting the interval. Given bio-oil’s density and limited mobility, leakage through a fault system is unlikely.
Pressure margin / MASIP
The Maximum Authorized Surface Injection Pressure (MASIP) is set by the regulator as a function of depth and formation fracture resistance to protect USDWs. In Louisiana (Class V primacy), IMD/Office of Conservation establishes MASIP using formulas or fracture-gradient/step-rate testing; if a recompletion moves the interval shallower, a new MASIP is issued for that depth [5]. Our operations remain well below MASIP, providing a safety margin to avoid fracture initiation or propagation of the confining units.

The Basco 6 SWD’s currently perforated interval (black bars, far left) within the Miocene Formation, likely isolated from one another. The carbon:oxygen response, “sigma”, and spectral gamma ray values (blue, green and red colorfill) will inform the presence of bio-oil post-injection.
Dissolved Inorganic Carbon (DIC) and Baseline Geochemistry
Formation water chemistry provides a quantitative basis for DIC concentrations in the Basco 6 SWD reservoir interval (see table below). Alkalinity and bicarbonate were measured at 502 mg/L as HCO₃⁻, with carbonate below detection (< 2 mg/L) and pH at 7.7. At this pH, the carbonate system is dominated by bicarbonate. Converting the bicarbonate concentration to a carbon-equivalent yields a DIC concentration of approximately 100 mg/L as C.

Complementing this, isotopic analysis of dissolved inorganic carbon reported δ¹³C of DIC = +5.4‰ (VPDB). Together, these results establish both concentration and isotopic baseline conditions for dissolved inorganic carbon in the formation fluid.

Baseline hydrocarbon gas composition was also determined by ASTM D1946 analysis of downhole gas samples, with methane ranging from 0.045–1.2% v/v, carbon dioxide at ~0.06–0.08% v/v, and nitrogen and oxygen/argon comprising the balance. These data provide a quantitative hydrocarbon baseline for the reservoir environment and reflect trace amounts of hydrocarbon saturation. Cased hole logging (pulsed neutron) showed no significant gas saturation, consistent with these results. The concentrations are too low to indicate mobile or volumetrically meaningful hydrocarbons in the pore system.

Charm Bio-Oil
Charm produces bio-oil at its pyrolysis facility in Fort Lupton, CO. The facility has operated in an R+D capacity since December 2022 in order to develop Charm’s internal pyrolysis technology. Production of bio-oil for sequestration and carbon removal credit generation began on 8/12/24, at which point the facility became shared-use

Charm uses woody biomass sourced from government-funded wildfire mitigation and restoration projects in Colorado. Charm works with foresters to source the non-merchantable logs removed from the forest as a part of this mitigation work. As a result, woody biomass without a clear alternative disposal pathway is removed from fire-prone collection areas and pyrolyzed so that its biogenic carbon can be permanently sequestered as bio-oil.

AECN Bio-Oil
Charm offtakes finished bio-oil from AE Cote-Nord Canada Bioenergy Inc. (AECN), a Quebec manufacturer who operates a pyrolysis facility. Construction began in 2016 and the facility was commissioned in 2018. It is expected to operate through 2042 and produce approximately 871,200 metric tonnes of pyrolysis oil during that time. AECN’s pyrolysis plant is located next to the Arbec lumber mill, which provides the pyrolysis feedstock in the form of waste sawdust and shavings from lumber processing.

The production of this waste is incidental to the production of wood boards for construction, which is the mill’s primary source of revenue. Absent an offtaker, the historical fate of the mill residue has been on-site piling, as is evidenced by the fact that the mill has already accumulated a large pile of sawdust that is years old. Piled, rotting wood waste is a source of both CO2 and methane emissions, with a significant global warming potential. By sequestering AECN bio oil, Charm enables AECN to divert more of this ongoing waste stream away from creating emissions, instead permanently sequestering the biogenic gasses underground and interrupting the emissions associated with the input biomass.

Charm contracts with transport providers to deliver bio-oil to Charm’s pre-processing facility in El Dorado, KS. At this facility, the oil is introduced to our material tanks and treatment system, which was constructed/commissioned in 2023-4. This site allows Charm to treat the bio-oil prior to injection in order to ensure that it is fully compliant with the requirements for underground injection.

Injection at Vaulted
Once successfully pre-treated to ensure UIC/KDHE compliance, the oil may be trucked to Vaulted’s Great Plains Facility in Hutchinson, KS  for injection into an underground salt cavern. Following treatment, samples are taken for CHN, TAN, and Density testing and the oil is transported to the Vaulted injection facility. At that point, the oil is emplaced into the storage cavern. Vaulted is also contracted to monitor the emplacement cavern and provide Charm with MRV data from their monitoring equipment. The details of Vaulted’s injection infrastructure and monitoring equipment is detailed in their own PDD and recorded in the relevant sections below.

Injection at Basco 6
Once successfully pre-treated to ensure UIC/LDENR compliance, the oil may be trucked to Charm’s Basco 6 injection well in Evangeline Parish, LA for injection into an orphaned oil and gas reservoir. The Bio-Oil may be stored in tanks prior to injection into the sub-surface or injected directly from tanker trucks. When a batch of Bio-Oil is ready for injection, Charm will inject a small amount of brine into the formation to ensure pressure and formation access is achieved. Once injection into the subsurface has been established with brine, Charm will switch to the injection of Bio-Oil. As per regulations, Charm will continuously monitor the injection rate and surface injection pressures at the wellhead. Automated shut-downs are in place to ensure compliance with regulation. 

Bio-Oil will continue to be pumped through the injection pump until the tank(s) containing the batch are empty. After all pre-processed Bio-Oil is injected, Charm will switch back to the brine to flush all residual oil from the injection lines, pump, and wellbore to ensure all Bio-Oil is delivered into the formation. After this brine flush is complete, the wellbore will be shut-in and pressure will be monitored in preparation for the next Bio-Oil injection. The ongoing monitoring program for this injection well is detailed in Supplemental Doc 3 and Appendix 6.

Charm Bio-Oil
The biomass Charm sources are forest residues (green logs) harvested from government regulated and funded forest management projects with the goal of wildfire mitigation, amongst other co-benefits. Given the region’s history of recent, catastrophic wildfires and the State of Colorado’s prioritization of mitigation of wildfire hazards, this project would have taken place irrespective of Charm’s involvement

According to the contractor and the forester from the CSFS, the forest residues that Charm sources from this project are not suitable for use in sawmills and have very limited disposal options in the area. This can be partly due to the quality, type of tree species, and inability to access disposal options due to the lack of economic attractiveness. The forester in charge of this project has provided an affidavit acknowledging the lack of merchantability for this material in the fire-prone ecosystem.

As such, the biomass is considered to 1) serve no purpose and 2) have 0 counterfactual storage per Condition C1 in Section 3.2.2 and EC 14 in Section 2.2 of the Isometric Biomass Feedstock Accounting Module v1.2.

A complete characterization of the feedstock and assessment of the GHG storage counterfactual can be found in the Biomass Feedstock Information appendix 2 and 3 below.

Charm’s bio-oil production facility is located in an area of Fort Lupton, CO that is zoned I2 for heavy industrial activity. The immediate area hosts other, comparable heavy industrial applications including a large Halliburton location, a crane rental company (Maxim Crane Works), and a manufacturer of truck and van bodies (Summit Truck & Van Bodies). Given the uses of neighboring properties, it is reasonable to presume that Charm’s location would be used for comparable commercial activity. For this reason, only facility development work and equipment installation specific to Charm’s production operations are considered above the baseline for the property. Emissions impacts of operational activities are captured in the accounting of aggregate fuel usage and equipment embodied emissions for pyrolysis operations.

AECN Bio-Oil
The bio-oil Charm sources from AECN is produced using residues, primarily sawdust, generated by the Arbec lumber mill adjacent to AECN’s pyrolysis facility. By purchasing the oil, Charm creates demand that AECN meets by offtaking additional residues from the mill. AECN is jointly owned by the sawmill, Arbec, and wood harvester, Groupe Remabec.The pyrolysis facility was developed as a solution to the ongoing buildup of residues. 

This development was, in part, triggered by the closure of other businesses in the region who would have otherwise purchased this byproduct as an input for their own production. Due to the remote location of the mill, the development of economically viable offtake relationships with businesses outside the region is a challenge. This is evidenced by Arbec and Groupe Remabec’s decision to invest over $100 million CAD to build out an onsite pyrolysis facility, as well as the accumulation of a large pile of sawdust as sawmill operations continued without an offtaker for residues for a significant period of time. The AECN facility was the solution to this problem.

Because its primary revenue stream is the production of wood boards for construction, the mill has operated, creating sawdust and wood shavings absent AECN or other offtakers. There is clear evidence that the historical practice given this condition has been to create the sawdust pile that still exists onsite, so it is reasonable to conclude that these waste products would continue to accumulate in this fashion given no other intervention.

In such a scenario, existing literature suggests that the anaerobic conditions in the center of a pile of sawmill residues would result in the emission of methane, alongside CO2. The high global warming potential (GWP) of methane would likely result in total emissions with a higher GWP over a 15-year period than if all biogenic carbon were released (Pier and Kelly, 1997). Charm’s sequestration of the oil made by pyrolyzing this feedstock removes what would otherwise act as the source material for these emissions and permanently sequesters it underground after processing into pyrolysis oil.

A complete characterization of the feedstock and assessment of the GHG storage counterfactual can be found in the Biomass Feedstock Information appendix below.

The Basco 6 EW Well is a former saltwater disposal well located in Evangeline Parish, LA. It was drilled in 2011 and, while active, injected nearly 20,000 bbls of produced water before transitioning to orphan well status in 2016. Despite efforts to plug orphaned wells in Louisiana, their number has continued to grow. Between October 2023 and April 2024, the LDENR recorded a 12.4% increase in the number of orphaned wells statewide, with a total count of 4,785 wells. Given this state of affairs, the likely fate of the Basco 6 EW Well absent remediation by Charm to support bio-oil storage would be continued deterioration as an unplugged, orphaned oil and gas asset. Left in such a condition, these wells pose the risk of long-term environmental and economic harm to the surrounding ecosystems and communities.

Vaulted has completed their own PDD under the Isometric Biomass Geological Storage Protocol and their durability assessment is copied below:

“Vaulted’s Great Plains Facility stores the waste in sealed salt caverns 500+ feet
underground for permanent (10,000+ year) geologic storage. Neither leakage nor re-
emissions of the waste and its carbon content is expected.

Durability is expected to exceed much further than 10,000 years. This expectation is
based on a combination of direct measurement, and modeling. Vaulted employs a
monitoring program both during and post operation which includes regular testing for
mechanical integrity of the wells and cavern integrity. This monitoring program enables the
confirmation that the cavern is stable and does not have any subsurface leak pathways.
This testing is done when a new cavern is opened, during pre-injection, injection, and
post-injection operations. Vaulted monitors the displaced brine returned from the cavern
for any waste, which is then filtered out and recycled back into the injection stream.

The Great Plains Facility employs slurry injection into salt caverns. Salt is an impermeable
formation, thus making it an effective method for securely storing waste materials without
risk of leakage into surrounding environments. The caverns at the facility exist at depths
where waste is no longer buoyant; if the wells have integrity (confirmed using the same
techniques as described above) and are property plugged, waste is permanently
sequestered. Safe and durable sequestration of waste in salt caverns has been confirmed
in literature, including here, here, and here. To further reduce any risks, Vaulted injects at
minimal pressure (80 psig at the wellhead, which maintains total pressure – injection plus
hydrostatic – below 0.75 psi / ft to the cavern top). This pressure threshold maintains
cavern integrity (the triaxial stress capacity of salt is 1 psi/ft of depth).

In addition to direct measurement, the broader literature models durability of Vaulted’s
sequestration approach. Salt caverns have been identified in the literature as a viable
location for permanent storage of CO2.”

Charm’s durability assessment of Basco 6 is as follows:
The Lower Miocene sandstone formation ~3500’ below the Basco 6 wellhead has been characterized and assessed for the performance required to ensure durable storage of injected bio-oil.

The primary considerations in assessing durability were the sequestration reservoir thickness and reservoir quality relative to adjacent confining layers, demonstration of wellbore integrity, and lack of offset activity in the sequestration zone in the region. 

In the course of this assessment Charm determined that the pressure required to pump bio-oil into the formation would be consistent with a typical injection formation (normally pressured or below) without exceeding maximum allowable injection pressure, and that the formation brine density is sufficiently lower than that of bio-oil. This ensures that Charm can pump bio-oil under the regulatory requirements and that upon entry into the formation, the bio-oil will sink as predicted. In addition, Charm performed a mechanical integrity test to ensure casing integrity and performed an advanced cement analysis to ensure hydraulic seal from the formation and the wellbore. The wellbore is proven to be in good mechanical condition and the formation is of sufficient injectivity and storage size. 

Given the above, we are confident in our assessment that the Lower Miocene zone penetrated by the Basco 6 EW  possesses the characteristics required for bio-oil storage on a 1000+ year timeline. Notes on Charm’s Ledger software

In this section and elsewhere in the PDD, reference is made to the Ledger. This is a piece of proprietary software developed internally by Charm’s software team. Once a given batch of bio-oil comes into Charm’s custody, as defined by issuance of a Bill of Lading, scale ticket and invoice document as discussed earlier in this section, the batch becomes a “Lot” in the Ledger. 

The software is designed to measure and track Lots as they are split and combined, maintaining a clear mass balance and chain of custody as they move between transport modes (e.g. truck to railcar to truck) and are stored, batched, and treated along the pathway to injection. Once a given Lot, or batched combination of several split Lots, is injected it is then logged as a Removal. The data for a given Removal includes the process emissions associated with the Lot components, calculated on a mass balance basis which traces back to the original Lot. 

The Ledger also captures data from “site emissions”, described in Appendix 2, which are facility emissions that cannot be neatly mapped to a specific Lot (e.g. site electricity usage). When a Removal is completed, the CHN testing associated with that removal assigns it a C and CO2e content, from which both process emissions and either all or a portion of site emissions for the reporting period in which the Removal is claimed, are deducted in order to compute the Net CDR for a given Removal

Note: Data quality assurance is an ongoing process. The following is a list of measures currently place, which is sure to grow as operational experience demonstrates optimization opportunities:
Removals are calculated and removal data is managed within the Charm Ledger. This specialized piece of software improves data quality by
Ensuring that emissions factors and measurements are used consistently across all estimates
Ensuring that calculation methods are used consistently across all estimates
Ensuring consistent amortization of embodied and site emissions
Reducing human error by automating calculation work such as unit conversions
Reducing human error by minimizing the amount of manual entry required to generate estimates

Datapoints requiring manual entry by operators are minimized. Where they are necessary, operators are trained on specific entry procedures. Opportunities for error are further minimized by eliminating cumbersome/complex entry requirements wherever possible.

Justification for your choice of uncertainty approach: 
A Sensitivity Analysis has been carried out for each measured emissions source/parameter based on a Sample Calculation of one truckload of bio oil originated at AECN and transported to Kansas for Injection as well as the first Charm bio-oil injection in Kansas.

In the Sensitivity Analysis, the value of each source was arbitrarily increased by 20%, a value far higher than a reasonable expectation of measurement variance for any given parameter. These changes are compared against an estimated “Sample Removal” without this variance. Any parameter that, when increased by 20%, contributed to a change in the estimated removal that was greater than 1% of the original estimation was then subject to an Uncertainty Analysis, as specified in Section 2.5.7 of the Isometric Standard.

Environmental impacts need to be considered with respect to the “upstream” processes of bio oil feedstock harvest and pyrolysis, as well as the impacts of the injection site.

Biomass Feedstock and Pyrolysis

Charm Bio-oil
Charm has gone to significant lengths to ensure that both its biomass feedstock sourcing and pyrolysis processes reflect sustainable best practices and are in compliance with all applicable regulations. 

Before deciding to move forward with a supplier, Charm assesses the counterfactuals for any given biomass source to confirm there is no alternate near-term use case for the material. The forest residues that are used in this Project come from a state-managed and/or state-funded Fuels Reduction and Forest Restoration project. The project area showed signs of high risk factors for a catastrophic wildfire, thus warranting the Colorado State Forest Service a treatment plan that thins the forest to help increase its health and resilience. Catastrophic wildfires contribute to a mixture of hazardous air pollutants and release large quantities of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gasses into the atmosphere. By using the woody biomass (fuels) from these fuels reduction projects, Charm can support and reduce the risk by disposing or reducing the fuels in the forest floor while sequestering the biogenic carbon in this material.

Charm’s pyrolysis process is designed to recapture waste heat to dry biomass which minimizes the need for combustion of propane or other fuels. Charm has engaged a qualified emissions testing company to perform stack emissions testing and undergone a rigorous third party analysis of potential emissions from its biomass processing and pyrolysis emissions process. This review has confirmed that Charm’s facility emissions are below major air quality permit thresholds. 

AECN Bio-oil
The primary environmental impacts upstream of injection are related to the biomass feedstock used for pyrolysis and the process of pyrolysis itself. AECN has gone to significant lengths to ensure that both the biomass feedstock and the pyrolysis process reflect their dedication to sustainability. 

All of the wood used as pyrolysis feedstock is harvested from provincially-owned forests in accordance with rights granted under the Quebec Provincial Management Plan (PMP). The PMP is explicitly guided by “sustainable forest management objectives” which dictate the bounds of allowable harvest such that the ecological integrity of provincial forestland is maintained over time.

In addition, the Bureau de Normalisation du Québec, an official certification organization accredited by the Standards Council of Canada, has granted the biomass harvester a certificate of compliance with the SFI Forest Management Standard. 

The pyrolysis process itself has also been engineered with sustainability in mind. The facility construction was financed in part by Sustainable Development Technology Canada and Department of Natural Resources Canada. The facility is also certified by SGS Germany as compliant with the EU International Sustainability and Carbon Certification standard and the requirements of Renewable Energy Directive (RED) II.

Biomass Feedstock and Pyrolysis

Charm Bio-oil
Charm has gone to significant lengths to ensure that both its biomass feedstock sourcing and pyrolysis processes reflect sustainable best practices and are in compliance with all applicable regulations. 

Before deciding to move forward with a supplier, Charm assesses the counterfactuals for any given biomass source to confirm there is no alternate near-term use case for the material. The forest residues that are used in this Project come from a state-managed and/or state-funded Fuels Reduction and Forest Restoration project. The project area showed signs of high risk factors for a catastrophic wildfire, thus warranting the Colorado State Forest Service a treatment plan that thins the forest to help increase its health and resilience. Catastrophic wildfires contribute to a mixture of hazardous air pollutants and release large quantities of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gasses into the atmosphere. By using the woody biomass (fuels) from these fuels reduction projects, Charm can support and reduce the risk by disposing or reducing the fuels in the forest floor while sequestering the biogenic carbon in this material.

Charm’s pyrolysis process is designed to recapture waste heat to dry biomass which minimizes the need for combustion of propane or other fuels. Charm has engaged a qualified emissions testing company to perform stack emissions testing and undergone a rigorous third party analysis of potential emissions from its biomass processing and pyrolysis emissions process. This review has confirmed that Charm’s facility emissions are below major air quality permit thresholds. 

AECN Bio-oil
The primary environmental impacts upstream of injection are related to the biomass feedstock used for pyrolysis and the process of pyrolysis itself. AECN has gone to significant lengths to ensure that both the biomass feedstock and the pyrolysis process reflect their dedication to sustainability. 

All of the wood used as pyrolysis feedstock is harvested from provincially-owned forests in accordance with rights granted under the Quebec Provincial Management Plan (PMP). The PMP is explicitly guided by “sustainable forest management objectives” which dictate the bounds of allowable harvest such that the ecological integrity of provincial forestland is maintained over time.

In addition, the Bureau de Normalisation du Québec, an official certification organization accredited by the Standards Council of Canada, has granted the biomass harvester a certificate of compliance with the SFI Forest Management Standard. 

The pyrolysis process itself has also been engineered with sustainability in mind. The facility construction was financed in part by Sustainable Development Technology Canada and Department of Natural Resources Canada. The facility is also certified by SGS Germany as compliant with the EU International Sustainability and Carbon Certification standard and the requirements of Renewable Energy Directive (RED) II.

9/9/25
During the past several months, Charm has worked together with Isometric to identify and implement some MRV changes in order to better fit evolving on-the-ground operations. The changes are as follows:
Charm pyrolysis currently produces 3 distinct products: Bio char and two bio-oil fractions “oily” and “aqueous”. Initially, it was not clear whether or not we would be able to successfully modify pre-processing operations to successfully render the aqueous oil non-hazardous. For that reason, we did not allocate production emissions to the aqueous oil, as the risk that it would not end up being injectable was too significant. By mid-year we had successfully proved out this process. As a result, starting with Production Period 7, which covers June production, we began to allocate emissions to all 3 products. Aqueous oil produced before June 2025 has no production emissions associated with it as all emissions have already been allocated to biochar and “oily” bio-oil
AECN bio-oil is weighed on a calibrated scale at the origination point and prior to injection. As the material is transferred between vessels during processing, volume and density are used to estimate the mass as it is not practical to weigh the material in situ. This can create upstream discrepancies as there are often small deltas between the product of volume and density and the mass measured on a scale. While this can be reconciled on a small scale, scaling up removals also scales up the opportunity for error in these manual adjustments. To avoid this, upstream emissions are allocated to injection batches by subtracting the Mass of Bio Oil Injected from the estimated Input Mass. Because the Input Mass is an estimate, that loss estimate is increased by 25% in order to ensure removals are accounted for conservatively, as this increases the production and transport emissions allocated to a removal, while holding the Mass of Bio Oil Injected constant. The emissions are allocated against the oldest BOL first, in order of delivery to the KS pre-processing site
KS permits require salt to be added to bio-oil at a level that significantly super-saturates the material. As a result, large deposits of salt are left over in the blending tank when the injectate is removed. Site engineering resources are able to volumetrically estimate the mass of salt in the tank by measuring deposit depth. In addition, multiple rounds of samples have been taken which show that the residue ranges from roughly 75-85% salt, with the remainder composed of entrained bio-oil. From the April/May Reporting Period on, 70% (used as a conservative estimate) of the estimated mass of the remaining residue may be counted towards the total salt added to the subsequent injection batch, provided the tank is not cleaned in between batches. 
Over the course of processing and injecting bio-oil, Charm operators took full and empty weights of many IBC totes used to store finished bio-oil. This practice yielded a large dataset, which we used to create a conservative standard loss estimate. This was computed by taking the full and empty weights of 59 bio-oil totes and taking the average. That average was then increased by 25% to ensure that the estimate would be conservative. Rather than take the empty weight of each individual tote, this standard loss (which came out to 5.33kg) is assumed for each tote of “oily fraction” Charm bio-oil used. There will be an annual revision of this estimate to ensure it stays current with the physical character of Charm’s bio-oil.

Counterfactual Storage Eligibility
Please describe and attach any relevant evidence to demonstrate that your feedstock would have emitted the biogenic CO₂ to the atmosphere sooner than the required threshold period. If only a portion of your feedstock would have emitted the CO₂ after the threshold period, attach relevant evidence and confirm that you have incorporated the relevant calculation into your LCA.
The Arbec sawmill, which provides AECN the sawdust and shavings used in pyrolysis, has a pile of sawmill residues that has been on site for years. The sawmill accumulated the pile of residues as a result of the shutdown of local businesses who had purchased it in the past. AECN was developed as a solution to this issue of residue accumulation. The historic fate of these residues absent AECN or other offtakers has been accumulation in this large pile.

Pier and Kelly’s study of waste sawdust in the Tennessee Valley (Pier and Kelly, 1997), included as documentation with this PDD, shows that anaerobic conditions in the center of such piles lead to significant methane production.

In order to compute a conservative estimation of the counterfactual storage of piled sawdust, we assume that 1% of the biogenic carbon lost to decomposition is emitted as methane. In terms of the overall decomposition rate, studies of woody debris decay vary widely. However, Fasth et al. (2011) found an average of a 24% annual decay rate for smaller-sized particles in a study of 11 different tree species. This is confirmed by Blasdell’s research on the decay rates of forest litter — This found a median 50% residence time of 2.48 years, from which a comparable decay rate can be inferred.

Using this as a benchmark, the total CO2e released by decomposing wood within 15 years, from emission of CO2 and CH4, would exceed the total stored carbon of the wood. Additionally, at a 24% decay rate, after 50 years the piled woody material would have effectively decayed entirely. For this reason, we are assigning the sawmill residues used by AECN as a pyrolysis feedstock a counterfactual storage discount of 0. This calculation is in accordance with EC13 of the Biomass Feedstock Accounting module.

Embodied Emissions from Feedstock Transport – Charm
Data Sources: Vendor LCA, GREET, USEPA
For all truck transport, the Vehicle Embodied Emissions are calculated using the GREET 2023 emissions factors for Medium-and-Heavy-Duty (MHDV) Trucks and Trailers. The total value for the embodied emissions is divided by the expected useful life for a Heavy Duty compression ignition (diesel) engine established by US EPA Office of Transportation and Air Quality in 2016 for engines manufactured after 2004 at 435,000 miles. This yields a quantity of embodied carbon per mile traveled.
The trucks being used in this project are provided by a third-party vendor. Therefore, only the mileage driven to transport biomass used for Charm bio oil is considered.
The emissions factor is modified to include the average “deadhead” or unloaded journey distance for a vehicle of this type according to ATRI analysis
Where consumables are shared between Production and other functions outside of the system boundary, usage will be allocated appropriately as evidenced by pyrolyzer up-time and/or historical usage rates
Data Quality Assessment
GREET, ATRI, and the US EPA qualify as “reputable sources” given the Isometric Protocol’s definition of “A source that would be widely considered trustworthy based on the process undertaken (e.g., peer review) or origin of the information (e.g., government body).”

Carbon Content of Injected Bio Oil
Data Sources
Mass of Injectate will be determined by using an on-site truck scale that will be used to weigh the delivery truck prior to and after injection. A weigh station ticket will be produced to verify mass injected
Mass of Injected Bio Oil will be determined by subtracting the mass of salt and LCS used for a particular batch of injectate from the Mass of Injectate
Carbon content of the oil will be measured using Isometric Protocol Method A – Each injection batch will be sampled prior to the addition of salt and LCS and one sample will be sent to an analytic lab to measure the carbon content of that batch. 
Data Quality Assessment
The scale used at Vaulted’s on-site weigh station is properly calibrated and maintained by Vaulted and the result is adjusted for the maximum allowable error for a calibrated legal-for-trade truck scale to ensure a conservative estimate of removals as detailed in the Uncertainty Analysis
All additions to the injectate are monitored using an on-site scale (solid additions) and flow meter (liquid additions) for each batch
Carbon content is measured by analytic testing at an ISO-certified lab and based directly on the batch being injected. The result is adjusted for the maximum allowable error for lab analysis to ensure a conservative estimate of removals as detailed in the Uncertainty Analysis

Data Sources

Biomass is often stockpiled and progressively used over a long period of time. Biomass transport emissions may be allocated to bio-oil based on the quantity of raw biomass (logs) consumed during a given period – Usage rates are monitored by measuring the linear feet of raw logs before and after processing. The appropriate proportion of total transport emissions will then be allocated to the oil produced during that period
Justification of Variables and Emissions Factors Used
Vehicle Type is always a Heavy Duty Vehicle (flatbed truck) due to the quantity and nature of cargo
Google Maps routing is a reliable source of mileage information and is controlled for uncertainty as described in the Uncertainty Analysis section

GLEC and Auburn University qualify as a “reputable source” for emissions factors given the Isometric Protocol’s definition
Use of linear feet to monitor the progressive consumption of a biomass stockpile is preferable to mass measurements as it avoids the uncertainty involved in estimating moisture and process losses during the processing steps required to prepare biomass for pyrolysis
Unit/Frequency of Calculation
Transport emissions are calculated for each delivery of biomass to Charm’s facility
When biomass is stockpiled, all deliveries composing the stockpile are summed to compute total transportation emissions for the stockpile
The biomass team also measures and records to total linear feet of logs composing the biomass stockpile once fully delivered
Once a biomass stockpile begins supplying production, the remaining linear feet are measured and recorded for each Production Period – The proportion of total transport emissions corresponding to the proportion of biomass consumed is allocated across the bio oil produced during that period
When a biomass stockpile is shared between Production and other functions outside of the system boundary (R+D), usage will be allocated appropriately as evidenced by pyrolyzer up-time
Delivery emissions may also be allocated to pyrolysis products as the deliveries occur
Emissions are allocated to pyrolysis products produced during the Production Period during which the delivery occurs
Modifications to Address Uncertainty
The mass used to determine delivery emissions is adjusted for the maximum allowable uncertainty for a legal-for-trade truck scale to ensure a conservative estimate of removals as detailed in the Uncertainty Analysis

Electricity Use from Biomass Pyrolysis – Charm
Calculation Methodology
Electricity emissions are calculated by kWh usage multiplied by the per kWh emissions for the RMPA grid subregion as reported by the EPA eGrid emissions factors and adjusted by Green-e to show the residual grid mix. This is supplemented by a per-kWh addition to account for the embodied emissions associated with grid infrastructure as quantified by a weighted average of power generation sources based on the fuel mix for the subregion reported by the EPA and the applicable electricity infrastructure emissions factors reported by GREET
Estimated general and administrative electricity use, based on historical usage rates, is subtracted from the total facility electricity usage. The difference between total facility usage and G+A is then allocated between production and R+D pyrolysis based on each process’s proportion of total pyrolyzer up-time during a given Production Period
Where RECs are used, the grid emission factor is replaced by an emission factor for the renewable generation type sourced from GREET for every kWh matched by a REC
The volume of consumption matched by each REC is reduced by a derating factor which is equal to the grid-region T+D losses quantified by USEPA eGrid
Justification of Variables and Emissions Factors Used
Electricity use is measured using a calibrated instrument from the local utility provider calibrated and purpose-built for measuring power usage over time. Given that the readings are the basis for billing for power usage, the utility has a direct incentive to ensure their accuracy
Subregional grid emissions factors and generation technology mix are provided by the US EPA and grid infrastructure emissions factors from GREET, which are reputable sources under the Isometric Protocol’s definition 
Estimating general and administrative power consumption based on historical estimates is inherently conservative given that the number of general and administrative functions supported by the facility has grown over time
Pyrolyzer up-time is logged by pyrolyzer operators as part of their standard operating procedure – Pyrolyzer operators are best positioned to log the start and end time of production runs
The RECs calculation approach used is aligned with the approach provided in the Isometric Energy Use Accounting module
Unit/Frequency of Calculation
At the close of a Production Period, total facility usage during the Period is pulled from the utility’s customer portal and uploaded to the Ledger where the historical usage estimate and allocation between Production and R+D is computed
Up-Time is logged for each production run and regularly uploaded to the Ledger
These emissions are assigned to bio oil produced during the Production Period during which the delivery occurs 
Modifications to Address Uncertainty
Meter readings are adjusted for uncertainty based on the maximum allowable uncertainty for a utility-provided electricity meter provided by the Code of Colorado Regulations

Stack Emissions from Pyrolysis – Charm
Calculation Methodology
The GHG composition and flow rate of pyrolysis tailgas is characterized for each type of feedstock processed using the results of compliance emissions testing performed by a qualified emissions testing company 
This data is used by Charm’s engineering team to calculate a quantity of tailags emitted per kg of biomass processed. This quantity is then scaled based on the biomass throughput recorded for production
The total methane released as a result of these emissions is converted to CO2e using the 100-year GWP of Methane quantified by IPCC AR-6
Justification of Variables and Emissions Factors Used
The testing company’s approval by an appropriate oversight authority to perform compliance emissions testing qualifies it as an acceptable source of such data per the Isometric Bio-Oil Geological Storage Protocol Section 7.4.3.5
Charm’s engineering team has unique process-specific knowledge required for a reasonable estimate of the relationship of the tailgas data provided by the emissions testing company to the emissions rate of a pyrolyzer as tested
100-year GWP based on the most recent volume of the IPCC Assessment Report is specified by the Isometric Standard for CO2e conversions
The Isometric Bio-Oil Geological Storage Protocol Section 7.4.3.5 specifies that tailgas emissions should be calculated in terms of the total release of methane
Unit/Frequency of Calculation
Tailgas emissions will be computed based on the quantity of biomass consumed by production pyrolyzers during a given Production Period. 
Emissions are allocated to pyrolysis products produced during the Production Period when production occurs
Modifications to Address Uncertainty
Total tailgas emissions are adjusted for uncertainty based on the measurement uncertainty reported for the emissions testing performed on the type of  feedstock processed
Embodied Emissions from Equipment and Facilities for Biomass Pyrolysis – AECN
Calculation Methodology
The bio oil vendor has not performed an evaluation of the embodied emissions associated with the construction of their building or manufacture of their equipment. Therefore, the best option available to Charm for determining these impacts is to use a cost-based calculation. This is achieved by applying per-dollar-spent supply chain emissions factors to the cost of construction, equipment, and installation and dividing that total by the expected lifetime production of the facility to arrive at a quantity of CO2e per MT of oil produced associated with facility embodied emissions. 
Justification of Variables and Emissions factors used
The Vendor is the best available source for cost information on the build of their facility. The figure provided exceeds what has been described in the public record, ensuring a conservative estimate of impacts using a cost-based model
NAICS Supply Chain Greenhouse Gas Emissions Factors are provided by the US EPA which qualifies as a “reputable source” given the Isometric Protocol’s definition
Unit/Frequency of Calculation
This will be applied as a static value against each MT of bio-oil delivered
Modifications to Address Uncertainty
This value is part of the overall “Embodied Emissions” for the purchased bio oil. This value is being used as is due to demonstrating a less than 1% change in the Net Removal in the Sensitivity Analysis. See the “Sensitivity Analysis” sheet in the supplemental GHG Statement doc

Additional Summary: Lot Emissions vs. Site Emissions
Overview: Emissions associated with bio-oil sequestration are calculated one of two ways.
Lot Emissions are emissions that can be clearly associated with a particular lot of bio oil. These emissions are associated with a specific lot of oil in the ledger and deducted from the net CDR associated with that specific lot or injection batch of bio-oil.
Site Emissions are emissions that cannot be associated with a specific lot of oil, but are associated with a particular processing site. These emissions are accrued to the site and deducted from removals associated with the site each reporting period. Site Emissions include, but are not limited to, emissions from energy used to power the site overall as these emissions cannot be reliably mapped to a particular processed lot.

All emissions (process and embodied) are aggregated at the site level and allocated across all pyrolysis products produced during a given Production Period
This means that they are calculated at the site level during production. However, when the emissions associated with a particular lot of Charm-produced bio-oil are used in the net CDR calculation of a particular removal, they are treated as lot emissions because they are associated with a particular bio-oil lot
This distinction also relates to periodization
A Production Period designates all of the CO2e emitted and pyrolysis products produced during a particular period at Charm’s pyrolysis facility produced between a start date and end date
All emissions produced during a given Production Period are allocated to the injectable pyrolysis products produced during that period
As injectable pyrolysis products are sequestered, the allocated emissions are deducted from the Net CDR associated with the removal. This calculation is made during the Reporting Period during which the injection occurs
A Reporting Period designates the reporting of all removals completed between a start and end date and the reporting of associated CDR calculations
Emissions associated with the production of pyrolysis products are netted out against the Net CDR claimed during the Reporting Period during which they are injected

Charm biochar is co-produced with bio-oil using the pyrolysis facility detailed in v1.8 of the “Charm Industrial Kansas Bio-Oil Sequestration Project Design Document”.

A summary of the pyrolyzer specifications is below. Figure 1a depicts the detailed design of the pyrolyzer including locations of sensors. Figure 1b depicts engineering drawings of the reactor subsystem. 

Reactor type: Updraft gasifier (fixed bed continuous operation)
Scale: 83kg of biomass consumed per hour of operation per machine
Final products: Bio-oil and biochar

Risk of release of pyrolysis gasses is assessed to be de minimus. In lieu of a detailed chemical kinetic model, Charm protects against release of pyrolysis gases via continuous pressure measurement and operating pyrolyzers at sub-atmospheric pressure as per section 9.1.2 of the biochar production and storage protocol. 

System pressure is monitored using differential pressure (DP) sensors. The locations of DPs are detailed in Figure 1a. The SCADA system used to automate pyrolyzer operation collects measurements from the DPs at 60hz. If the system differential pressure is detected to be outside of the nominal operating range (-2 to -1.2 millibar) the SCADA system will immediately and automatically shut down the system. Raw data from the SCADA system are recorded in local and cloud storage. DPs are calibrated weekly.

Charm pyrolyzers are designed for safe and reliable operation. They are constructed of the following materials:
The reactor subsystem consists of an A36 grade steel weldment lined internally with ceramic. It operates at a max temperature of 800C. The ceramic lining is rated up to 1760C. 
All other components that interface with the process stream are constructed from stainless steel


Consideration for high pressure conditions is not required. The pyrolyzers are designed for 5 years of safe and reliable operation. All mechanical components are designed to last for at least 5 years under regular maintenance. During operation the SCADA system executes a set of critical safety checks at 60hz. These checks include monitoring system temperatures and pressures. If any safety checks fail, or if connection is lost to any critical sensors or subsystems, the SCADA system will immediately and automatically shut down the system.  

Charm pyrolyzers are frequently inspected to ensure that they are suitable for safe and reliable operation. Each pyrolyzer is operated continuously for up to 48 hours. A period of continuous operation is referred to as a “hotflow”. Following each hotflow the pyrolyzers enter a “turnaround” during which they are cooled, cleaned, and visually inspected to ensure thermal and mechanical integrity. During turnaround the maintenance team makes repairs and replaces parts as needed. The reactors operate at very slightly subatmospheric pressure so requirements regarding maintenance of pressure vessels are not applicable to this project. 

The 2 co-products from Charm pyrolyzer operations are bio-oil and biochar. The bio-oil is stored and prepared for injection as detailed in v1.8 of the “Charm Industrial Kansas Bio-Oil Sequestration Project Design Document”. The biochar is stored and prepared for sequestration via application to soils. 

The non-condensable pyrolysis gasses are co-fired with propane in a burner that provides heat to the systems used to dry biomass feedstock in preparation for pyrolysis. Emissions of non-CO2 GHGs from the pyrolysis gas combustion system are estimated via emissions testing as detailed in Appendix 3 of v1.7 of the “Charm Industrial Kansas Bio-Oil Sequestration Project Design Document”.

Heavy metal content of biochar used as an agricultural soil amendment is addressed in the Conservation Practice Standard for Soil Carbon Amendment mained by the U.S. Department of Agriculture – Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS). Table 1 in Figure 2 shows the NRCS limits for heavy metals except the limit for Cr which is listed in Table 3. Figure 3 shows a 3rd party analysis of Charm biochar performed by Soil Control Labs. All heavy metals are within NRCS Conservation Practice Standard limits. 

PAH content in biochar used as an agricultural soil amendment is subject to oversight by NRCS and by the US EPA. NRCS guidance states that biochar should not be produced using feedstocks that are likely to contain elevated levels of organic contaminants, notably including municipal wastes. Charm complies with this guidance by exclusively using softwoods harvested from Colorado forests.
 
Charm pyrolyzers limit PAH accumulation in biochar by design. The reactor uses automated controls to maintain pyrolysis temperatures above 500C and pyrolysis vapors are continuously evicted from the reactor. Both of these features serve to limit the condensation of pyrolysis vapors within the reactor, preventing accumulation of PAHs in biochar.

In order to further minimize the risk to environmental and human health, Charm will voluntarily take the additional precaution of constraining the rate of biochar application to agricultural soils to no more than 4 tonnes/acre. 

US EPA guides evaluation of environmental and health risks from PAHs in agricultural soil amendments based on a combination of PAH content in the amendment and bioavailability of PAHs in the amendment. 

Figure 4a shows a 3rd party analysis from BSK Associates showing concentrations of the 16 USEPA priority PAHs. 15 of the 16 are not detectable at a reporting limit of 200 ug/kg. The concentration of the 1 remaining PAH, napthalene, is 250 ug/kg. The bioavailability of PAHs in Charm biochar is unknown as no standard method exists for measuring it. In a conservative worst-case estimate we can assume that 100% of PAHs in Charm biochar are bioavailable. Under these assumptions the application of Charm biochar to agricultural soils meets the US EPA definition of low risk to human and environmental health. 

These contaminants are subject to oversight by the NRCS and by the US EPA. EHS risks at the biochar production facility:
Fire
Ignition is a significant risk in biochar production. Biochar exits the reactor at upwards of 600C. At these temperatures biochar can ignite in the presence of oxygen. 
In order to prevent ignition during biochar production, biochar is conveyed from the reactor using a series of sealed augers into an airtight steel drum. 
In order to prevent ignition when biochar is transferred from steel drums into vinyl bags, the drums are stored for 24-72 hours in order to allow the biochar to cool. During the transfer process the team further mitigates ignition risk by adding water until the biochar reaches 30% moisture content. 
In order to prevent ignition when biochar is stored, biochar is stored in an isolated area away from any spark or flame sources.  
Procedures for incident response in the event of a fire are detailed in Charm’s Emergency Action Plan. 
Biochar fires are considered reportable safety incidents. Safety incidents are reported using an anonymous online portal. Safety incident reports are monitored and managed by Charm’s EHS team. 
Skin contact and dust
In order to minimize the risk of health impacts from biochar contact with skin, eyes, ingestion or inhalation, operators at the production site are required to wear PPE when handling biochar. This includes dust masks or respirators, eye protection, long sleeves, long pants, close toed shoes, and gloves. 
The risk of dust formation is further mitigated via the addition of water during the consolidation process. 
Spills
Biochar spills do not present a material risk to human safety or the environment. Charm conducts analytical testing to ensure that biochar is safe for application to agricultural soils. Biochar spills are not considered reportable safety incidents. 
The vinyl bags used for biochar storage are tied shut in order to minimize the risk of spills or attrition during handling and storage.  
In the event of a spill, operators at the site shovel the spilled material into vinyl bags. 
Small volumes of biochar may remain on the soil surface after spill cleanup. For example there are small volumes of biochar scattered on the ground at Charm’s biochar storage site. This spilled material does not present a material risk of leaching toxins into the soil. Charm conducts analytical testing to ensure that biochar is safe for application at rates of several tons per acre. The small spilled volumes represent small fractions of that application rate.

Sampling Guidance, Laboratory Requirements, and Data Quality 
Please describe and justify the sampling procedure and number of analyses based on the project-specific considerations, following Section 3.4  of the Biochar Storage in Agricultural Soils . 
Charm’s biochar and bio-oil production are divided into periods of time referred to as Production Periods. Within a Production Period, Charm will use a consistent biomass feedstock, consistent process for pre-processing biomass, consistent pyrolyzer operation setpoints, and consistent process for post-processing biochar. An example Production Period would be the month of January 2025. Production periods are discussed further in Section B of the “Charm Industrial Kansas Bio-Oil Sequestration Project Design Document”. 

Charm will sample production batches as per Method A in section 8.3.1 of the biochar production and storage protocol. For each production batch, Charm will refer to EBC guidelines for collecting a representative composite sample. The procedure involves drawing small sub-samples throughout the Production Period, combining and blending the sub-samples into a composite sample, and extracting final replicates from the composite sample. The replicates will be submitted to 3rd party labs for analysis. All biochar in 1 production batch is produced using the same biomass feedstock, same pyrolyzers, and uniform pyrolysis process setpoints. 

Charm will submit 3 replicates per Production Period for analysis of C content, moisture content, and H:Corg. Charm has completed analysis of 1 replicate for analysis of O:Corg, volatile matter content, and ash content. Analysis results are reported in Figure 6. Charm works with Soil Control Labs for measuring C content, moisture content, H:Corg, O:Corg, ash content, and volatile content.

Charm will estimate mean average soil temperature using the most recent and spatially specific publicly available dataset. The most preferred method would be to use an average of direct year-round measurements from academic or government datasets collected near the biochar application site. If direct measurements are not available then Charm will fall back to estimating mean annual soil temperature using other Lembrechts et al. (2022)  or other equivalent sources from academic literature or government data sources. 

For each biochar storage site Charm will identify the data source and region that is most representative of the storage site location.

Field Management Practices  
Please describe the field management practices prior to the Project, following Section 4.1.2  of the Biochar Storage in Agricultural Soils . 
Charm is in the process of securing offtake agreements for biochar. Upon successful completion of an offtake agreement Charm will report the storage site locations and any available information regarding field management practices including cropping, irrigation, and tillage. The availability of this information is subject to the offtaker’s willingness to provide it. This information is not always available as some offtakers consider their field management practices to be proprietary information. 

The preferred location for biochar offtakes is in the direct vicinity of Charm’s biochar production site in Fort Lupton Colorado. In this region growers most commonly cultivate wheat, hay, and corn using reduced tillage. Irrigation and the use of fertilizers and other soil amendments are common practices. The usage of Charm biochar is not expected to induce any changes in farm management practices. 

In the United States biochar is a competitive market with many sellers and relatively few buyers. As a result Charm may have to sell biochar in locations further away from Fort Lupton. In each geography Charm will establish storage site boundaries and will assess field management practices subject to the availability of that information. 

For the purposes of validation Charm and the VVB conducted a site visit to observe biochar application at a field in Central Kansas. At that field the land managers reported the following management practices:
Annual planting of single-crop soybeans
1 plot irrigated using center-pivot, one plot dryland
No-till or vertical tillage
One-time application of urea fertilizer prior to planting

Site Selection
Sites for biochar application are screened on a case by case basis. Biochar has been demonstrated to deliver benefits in a wide variety of applications and settings. 

The primary criteria for applying biochar to a site is reaching agreement with the land manager that biochar may be expected to assist with the land manager’s soil health and agronomic objectives. Charm and land managers jointly consider a variety of criteria including cropping systems, seasonality, soil texture, soil carbon content, equipment availability, water inputs, and which soil amendments are used in the baseline scenario. 

In some cases Charm may determine that a site is not suitable for biochar application even if there are potential soil health or agronomic benefits. These determinations will be made on a case by case basis. Some cases that would require additional consideration include:
If the application site is known to have elevated concentrations of pollutants, further consideration is required to determine the impacts of biochar on pollutant concentrations. In some cases biochar can be an effective strategy for managing pollutants. These cases will be evaluated in collaboration with Isometric and with the relevant regulatory authorities. 
If the application site has an extreme risk of erosion (e.g. extreme slope, history of landslides), further consideration is required to determine whether the site is suitable. Biochar can improve soil aggregate stability which can help to manage erosion. However the risk of biochar attrition via erosion must also be considered. 

Storage Site Monitoring
Charm will monitor biochar storage sites in collaboration with biochar offtakers. At the conclusion of each growing season following biochar application Charm may contact the offtaker to collect information regarding land management practices and any impacts of biochar. 

Management practices of interest include cropping, irrigation, tillage, and usage of soil amendments. Measurements of interest include soil pH, SOC content, N/P/K content, water holding capacity, and crop yields per acre. Data will be requested for biochar amended plots and control plots. 

The availability of this information is subject to the offtaker’s willingness to provide it.
